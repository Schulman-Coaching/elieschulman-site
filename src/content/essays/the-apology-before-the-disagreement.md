“I’m sorry, but I read that differently.”

The disagreement has not yet been spoken. The apology is already there.

It may be courtesy. It may be an attempt to soften a sentence. We do not have to assign one meaning to it. But we can become curious about what the speaker expects to happen next.

Does having a different view feel like causing an injury? Is the apology meant to reassure the listener—or to obtain permission to continue?

**An apology can make room for a disagreement. It can also quietly describe the disagreement as an offense.** The same words may do either, depending on the people and the moment.

The person listening has a part in this too. We may say that we welcome disagreement and then become noticeably colder when it arrives. We may reward the person who agrees with warmth, attention, or relief. Other people learn from those reactions, including the ones we do not intend to teach.

Imagine pausing after the apology and asking, without accusation, “What are you sorry about?” The answer might be simple: “Nothing, really. It is just how I begin.” Or it might open something neither person had noticed.

In a group, the question is available to everyone. What happened in us when a different reading appeared? Did we become curious, impatient, protective, amused? Who hurried to reassure the speaker? Who wished they would get to the point?

We need not ban the apology or train ourselves to sound more confident. We can listen to the sentence we actually used, and ask what it was doing for us.
